Entry 01 — Jul 2026

Why I built Clawcruit: hiring shouldn't take weeks

Every founder hits the same wall eventually: a role needs filling, and filling it properly means weeks of job posts, interviews, and onboarding — while salary and opportunity cost run in the background regardless. That gap is what Clawcruit exists to close.

The idea is simple to say and harder to build well: describe a role, and get a working AI hire for it in about a minute, operating from a dashboard and from the chat tools your team already lives in — Slack, WhatsApp, Teams, wherever. Not another piece of software to adopt, but a hire that shows up where the work already happens.

The part I'm proudest of is the "sovereign brain" — three editable Markdown files that define identity, business knowledge, and role-specific workflow for each hire. It means the knowledge stays with the business, fully readable and editable, instead of vanishing into a black box.

Entry 02 — Jun 2026

What accounting taught me about running a venture

An Accounting & Finance foundation isn't the background people expect from someone founding an AI company, but it's turned out to be the most useful thing I carry into it. Every product decision is, underneath, a resource-allocation decision — and that's a language I already spoke before I wrote a line of product spec.

It shows up in small ways: pricing Clawcruit around usage rather than seats, because that's how the underlying compute cost actually behaves. It shows up in big ways too — knowing when a venture's numbers say "not yet" even when the enthusiasm says "now."

Entry 03 — Jun 2026

Building a season simulator: rules before randomness

When I started the Clawforge Premier League engine, the instinct was to reach for randomness first — roll a die, get a score. That produces results, but not a league that feels like it has a logic to it. The better approach: define the rules exhaustively before you let anything vary.

Concretely, that meant building team strength ratings, a fixture generator, and a match-result function in that order — each deterministic and testable on its own — before layering in the randomness that makes a season interesting to watch. Randomness applied on top of firm rules reads as drama. Randomness applied first just reads as noise.
